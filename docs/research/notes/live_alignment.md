# Real-time audio-to-audio alignment ("score following") for tracking a live band against a reference recording

Scope note: these notes cover the daemon-side tracker that would consume live audio from an external interface and emit `(position_seconds_in_reference, rate, confidence)` for the existing g3xn song clock (`follow(at, rate, running)`, `POST /api/stage`). Everything already built (tap clock, voice corrections, song map, cues, ghost band) is taken as given. Every number is labelled with the music it was measured on; almost all published evidence is classical piano/orchestra/opera, and that is itself a key finding (see the last section).

Digit-decoding note: the Kirchhoff & Lerch 2011 PDF extracted with a font cipher; its digits were decoded with the mapping `[`=1 `]`=2 `‘`=3 `a`=4 `b`=5 `c`=6 `d`=7 `e`=8 `f`=9 `Z`=0 `W`=`.`, verified against known values ("2005", "440 Hz", "22 pianists"). Numbers from that paper are marked (decoded).

## Q1. Algorithm families for online audio-to-audio alignment, and which features / frame rates work for a full-band mix

### Takeaway
Online time warping (OLTW; Dixon 2005, refined by Arzt & Widmer) is the only family with both 20 years of live-concert use and maintained Python code; it tolerates tempo differences natively (path slope constrained to 1/3..3) but not structural jumps. Particle filters (Montecchio & Cont; Korzeniowski et al.) model position and tempo jointly and can start from an unknown position; HMM followers were "sticky" and clearly worse than OLTW in the only side-by-side Python benchmark; learned/neural features have no real-time evidence at all. The feature consensus across every source is chroma (12 pitch classes) plus an onset-emphasising spectral-difference feature, computed with ~46 ms windows and 20-23 ms hops (43-50 frames/s), tracked inside a 5-10 s search band; a second, coarse feature stream at 600 ms / 300 ms is used for acquisition and lost-detection.

### Cited Findings
- OLTW (Dixon 2005): incremental DTW with linear time/space; a search width `c` of cells per new row/column; `MaxRunCount = 3` bounds the path slope to between 1/3 and 3 ("to allow for moderate but not arbitrary differences in tempo"); features are the positive spectral difference between successive 20 ms frames (energy increases per bin, emphasising onsets) compared by Euclidean distance; with c = 500 frames (10 s) it "aligned each minute of music in about 10 seconds (3GHz PC)" offline and "runs comfortably in real time". On 22 pianists x 2 Chopin excerpts, average error 84 ms online vs 30 ms offline; worst 2.62 s online; 78.4% of notes within 100 ms and 92.7% within 200 ms online (Etude) — [Dixon, IJCAI 2005](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2005/ijcai05.pdf)
- The DAFx 2005 version reports a median error of 40 ms online vs 20 ms offline on classical piano, and that "the centre of the search band is adaptively adjusted to follow the best match" — [DAFx 2005 paper page](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/details/o-kAKlRZ1QVrNYxd28nF0g)
- MATCH (the offline/online toolkit built on OLTW): Hamming window 46 ms (2048 points), hop 20 ms, frequency axis mapped to a semitone-ish scale; "a lower frame rate could be used without significant loss of precision"; alignment of two files took ~4% of their summed duration in Java on a 3 GHz PC (2005) — [Dixon & Widmer, ISMIR 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf)
- Arzt & Widmer's production tracker: high-resolution features = windowed FFT, Hamming 46 ms, hop 20 ms, mapped to 84 semitone-spaced bins (linear below 370 Hz, log above), keeping only the per-bin energy increase; low-resolution features = the same sequence convolved with a 30-frame (600 ms) Hann window, downsampled by 15 to a 300 ms hop, each vector normalised to sum 1; Euclidean distance; ODTW with c = 500, MaxRunCount = 3, DTW weights wa = 1 (horizontal/vertical) and wb = 2 (diagonal) — [Arzt & Widmer, STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf)
- The 2015 version of the same tracker uses "a mix of chroma features and 'semi-tone onset' features" with an adaptive distance normalisation (Arzt, Widmer & Dixon, EUSIPCO 2012) — [Arzt & Widmer, ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Brazier & Widmer's opera tracker (audio-to-audio, reference = a commercial CD): MFCC features, 20 ms window, 10 ms hop, cosine distance, search interval c = 4000 frames (40 s of context), linear time per frame; low-resolution stream from a 600 ms Hann / 300 ms hop as in Arzt 2010 — [Brazier & Widmer, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Particle-filter family: Montecchio & Cont formulate alignment "as the state tracking of a dynamical system" whose hidden state (position, tempo) is continuous rather than discrete, and handle "both problems of audio-to-score and audio-to-audio alignment within the same framework in a real time setting" — [Montecchio & Cont, ICASSP 2011 (HAL record)](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00692579). Their ISMIR 2011 follow-up uses state x = (s, t) with s = position in seconds and t = speed ratio, observation likelihood = KL divergence between power spectra, Gaussian transition with separate variances for tempo drift and local mismatch, the "condensation" sampling scheme, and a uniform prior over the whole reference when the start is unknown; 60 particles per second of reference; "aligns a minute of audio in 2.29 s for Ns = 10^5 on a laptop computer with a 2.4 GHz Intel i5 processor (a single core is used)" — [Montecchio & Cont, ISMIR 2011](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2011/paper/000028.pdf)
- Korzeniowski, Krebs, Arzt & Widmer's DBN/Rao-Blackwellised particle filter: 50 ms hop (2205 samples at 44.1 kHz), STFT 4096 Hann; three feature streams: tonal content, a spectral-flux onset function, and loudness (SPL); tempo stored as log2(bpm) and updated only at note onsets; MAP position = mean of the top 20% particles by weight — [Korzeniowski et al., ICMC 2013](https://phenicx.upf.edu/system/files/publications/Korzeniowski_ICMC_2013.pdf)
- HMM vs OLTW head-to-head (matchmaker, classical piano, STFT chroma at 30 fps): on (n)ASAP, OLTWArzt MAE 91 ms / AAE 184 ms / 84.8% within 500 ms; OLTWDixon MAE 97 ms / 82.5%; HMM MAE 346 ms / 37.5%. The HMM "is 'sticky' and can linger on sustained notes"; the framework "lacks tempo models for HMM-based followers". Per-frame feature cost (averaged over an i9-9900K, M4 Mac mini, M2 Pro MacBook): chroma 3.05 ms, mel 3.40 ms, CQT 42.58 ms, LSE (log-spectral energy, onset-sensitive) 0.91 ms, MFCC 2.58 ms; online alignment step: OLTWDixon 1.22 ms, OLTWArzt 0.07 ms (Cython), HMM 3.59 ms; MAE by feature: LSE 242 ms, chroma 266 ms, mel 298 ms, CQT 341 ms, MFCC 932 ms; "LSE and OLTWArzt give the best balance of accuracy and low latency" — [Park, Cancino-Chacón, Kwon & Nam, ISMIR 2025 / arXiv 2510.10087](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10087v1)
- Feature study for audio-to-audio alignment (offline DTW; 62 features in envelope / timbre / pitch / onset groups; hop 23 ms, 46 ms frequency-domain frames; test set = 30 s clips time-stretched by factors in 3/4..4/3 with a 1/3 Hz triangular tempo modulation, plus formant/pitch/timbre edits; signals include singers, solo strings/winds, orchestra+choir, string quartet, wind quintet, voice+piano, speech): pitch features "perform better than the remaining feature groups regarding both accuracy and robustness"; best pitch chroma mean absolute deviation 16.9 ms (decoded), worst pitch feature 40.7 ms, lowest maximum error 0.56 s (low-pass-filtered chroma); best envelope feature 83.2 ms mean with ~3.7 s maximum deviation ("a weak property to synchronize audio files"); best onset feature (first difference of power) 22.7 ms. On real Chopin recordings (22 pianists): average error envelope 202.5/77.6 ms, timbre 182.8/122.1 ms, onset 32.5/38.9 ms, pitch 35.9/35.1 ms, onset+pitch combined 24.9/28.2 ms (Etude/Ballade), maximum error onset+pitch 1.87/3.66 s; timbre features failed on repeated motifs ("aligned different versions of these motifs"). The chroma variant using trapezoid filters with a 30-cent flat zone was designed for recordings whose tuning is not exactly 440 Hz — [Kirchhoff & Lerch, JNMR 2011](https://musicinformatics.gatech.edu/wp-content_nondefault/uploads/2015/04/Kirchhoff_Lerch_2011_Evaluation%20of%20Features%20for%20Audio-to-Audio%20Alignment.pdf)
- Performance-to-performance (offline) feature study on orchestra / solo guitar / solo piano CDs: pitch-oriented features with high frequency resolution (MFCC with 50 coefficients on a 372 ms FFT, and CQT) separated matching from non-matching frames best (highest Jensen-Shannon divergence); on the solo-guitar set MFCC "shows a substantial reduction in JSD ... a consequence of the sensitivity of the MFCC features to the guitar tuning" — [Grachten, Gasser, Arzt & Widmer, ISMIR 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000158.pdf)
- Offline reference pipeline (synctoolbox notebook): `Fs = 22050`, `feature_rate = 50`, chroma from pitch features plus DLNCO (decaying locally-adaptive normalised chroma onset) features, MrMsDTW `step_weights = [1.5, 1.5, 2.0]`, `threshold_rec = 10**6` — [sync_audio_audio_full.ipynb](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/groupmm/synctoolbox/master/sync_audio_audio_full.ipynb)
- Learned features: the Siamese-CNN frame-similarity approach reports higher accuracy than handcrafted-feature DTW but is explicitly "offline audio-to-score alignment of piano music" — [Agrawal & Dixon, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.07546v1); a 2025 neural score follower for computer accompaniment (HeurMiT) was reported by its own author as not practical "in real-world score following scenarios" — [Pillay, 2025](https://pdf.arxiv.org/abs/2503.06348); matchmaker's authors state learned or multimodal features are not evaluated and that other instruments/genres "would require new datasets and feature modules" — [arXiv 2510.10087](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10087v1)
- For singing, a review of a real-time lyrics-alignment system reports that chromagrams alone "were not effective for singing" in classical vocal performance (secondary source) — [themoonlight.io review](https://themoonlight.io/review/a-real-time-lyrics-alignment-system-using-chroma-and-phonetic-features-for-classical-vocal-performance)

### Inferences
- For a pop/rock band mix, use the two-stream feature set every production tracker converged on: (a) 12-bin chroma (L1-normalised, log-compressed) for harmonic content and (b) a half-wave-rectified spectral-difference feature folded to 12 semitone classes (DLNCO-like) or the matchmaker "LSE" for onsets. Both are a few numpy lines on a 2048-point STFT at 44.1 kHz (46 ms window, 20-23 ms hop, i.e. 43-50 fps); no library beyond numpy is required.
- Latency budget of the feature stage alone is ~70-90 ms (46 ms window + 20 ms hop + a 1024-2048-sample input block). The causal forward path adds systematic error on top of that: Dixon's online/offline gap (84 vs 30 ms average; 40 vs 20 ms median) and matchmaker's online MAE of ~90-150 ms on piano are the realistic floor; expect 100-300 ms position error on a good day.
- Window sizes: 5-10 s (250-500 frames at 50 fps) for the fine tracker is the universal choice; Brazier's 40 s band is for opera with huge tempo freedom and is overkill here. A 10 s band at 50 fps is 500 cells x 24 dims per frame — negligible CPU.
- Drop CQT (14x the cost of chroma, worse accuracy) and plain MFCC (worst accuracy online; tuning-sensitive on guitar). Avoid torch/learned features: no evidence of real-time benefit and the laptop's spare CPU is better reserved for faster-whisper.

### Gaps
- No published online alignment numbers exist for a pop/rock band mix; every frame-rate/feature recommendation above is transferred from classical piano/orchestra/opera.
- No source quantifies how much vocals (as opposed to instruments) degrade chroma-based alignment in a band mix; only the secondary classical-vocal remark was found.

## Q2. Concrete Python tools: matchmaker, synctoolbox, libfmp, librosa, other OLTW code; Antescofo-like systems; torch on a laptop CPU

### Takeaway
`pymatchmaker` (CP-JKU/KAIST, Apache-2.0, v0.4.0 released 2026-10-27, Python 3.10-3.13) is the only maintained real-time alignment library in Python and ships Cython OLTW (Dixon, Arzt, Arzt+tempo, multi-fold), HMM, and `skf` / `pfkorz` followers (presumably switching-Kalman and a Korzeniowski-style particle filter; the names are not expanded in any source read) — but its public API is audio-to-score (score synthesised with FluidSynth), it has no Windows wheel and no Windows CI, no confidence output, and it drags in partitura/pyfluidsynth/parangonar. `synctoolbox` (MIT, 1.4.2, May 2026) and `libfmp` (MIT, 1.3.0, March 2026) are actively maintained, pure-Python/numba, and offline; they are the right tools for pre-computing the reference (album-to-rehearsal map, tuning offset, chroma shift). `librosa.sequence.dtw` is offline but has subsequence mode. No other OLTW package exists on PyPI. Antescofo is IRCAM/Max, not Python. Nothing supports a torch-based tracker on a laptop CPU.

## Q3. Tempo drift vs structural jumps: which methods tolerate which, and how practical systems detect "lost" and re-acquire

### Takeaway
Any DTW/OLTW tolerates tempo drift within its slope constraint (1/3..3 with MaxRunCount = 3) and Arzt's tempo model removes most of the residual error by stretching the reference on the fly; a band's +/-10% is trivial. Structural deviations are the real killer: a plain band-limited OLTW "gets lost at the first score jump" and never recovers, offline fixes (JumpDTW, NWTW) need the whole recording, and the only online solutions are (a) JumpDTW-style allowed transitions at known part boundaries (your song map) and (b) a parallel coarse "rough position estimator" that scores every position in the song from the last ~9 s of audio and re-seeds the fine tracker when it is confident — with re-acquisition taking ~5-15 s. Confidence in practice = path-slope plausibility, accumulated-cost comparisons between competing hypotheses, and agreement between the coarse and fine trackers.

### Cited Findings
- Tempo: OLTW's MaxRunCount = 3 "embodies the assumption that a given performance will not be more than 3 times faster or slower than the reference"; performance "decreases with extreme tempo changes within a performance, and with large differences in absolute tempo between the (audio representation of) the score and the live performance. Problems are especially apparent at phrase boundaries" — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf)
- Arzt's simple tempo model: tempo = slope of a rectified backward path over the n = 20 most recent onsets that are at least 1 s old, local slope in a 3 s window, recency-weighted mean; the estimate is used to "stretch or compress" the reference feature sequence on the fly by probabilistically removing or inserting (mean) feature vectors, at most 3 alterations in a row; this cut the misalignment rate (>250 ms) from 41.8% to 23.6% (Schubert Impromptu), 31.8% to 17.1% (Rachmaninoff), 27.6% to 16.3% (Chopin Waltz), overall precision 83.2% -> 89.7% (91.1% with learned tempo curves from other performances); orchestral Beethoven 15.9% -> 15.0% misaligned; average errors 40-107 ms on correctly matched notes — [SMC 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_SMC_2010.pdf)
- A monotonically increasing stretch factor was deliberately included in the Kirchhoff & Lerch test set "to ensure that a completely diagonal path — the path preferred by standard DTW if the similarity matrix entries are very similar to each other — will not result in unreasonably good evaluation results" — [Kirchhoff & Lerch 2011](https://musicinformatics.gatech.edu/wp-content_nondefault/uploads/2015/04/Kirchhoff_Lerch_2011_Evaluation%20of%20Features%20for%20Audio-to-Audio%20Alignment.pdf)
- Structure kills band-limited OLTW: "if one performer repeats the first section of a movement and another performer does not, there is no way for the DTW algorithm to recover, since the width of the search band is only 5 or 10 seconds" — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf); in opera with 33-66% of parts removed plus one repeat, the baseline OLTW scored 10.4% part-wise accuracy and "gets 'lost' at the first score jump ... and never finds the right score position again" — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- JumpDTW (offline): the score is cut into blocks at structural indicators (double bar lines, repeat signs, segno/coda, section titles) and jumps "are allowed only at block boundaries but never inside blocks"; on 54 Beethoven sonata movements (312 min), classical DTW matched 74.6% of performance bars and 69.8% bar-level accuracy, JumpDTW 99.2-99.4% matched bars and 98.5-99.1% bar-level accuracy; chroma+DTW is the standard offline approach — [Fremerey, Müller & Clausen, ISMIR 2010](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2010_FremereyMuellerClausen_PartialSync_ISMIR.pdf)
- Needleman-Wunsch time warping (offline, no annotations): adds gap (skip) operations with penalty gamma = 0.346 plus 1-to-2 / 2-to-1 steps for tempo; on Mozart sonata pairs with structural differences 96.4% of beats within 500 ms (DTW 91.5%) and 91.7% within 100 ms (DTW 85.6%); without structural differences NWTW and DTW are equal (96.1% vs 95.4% within 100 ms); full-matrix computation feasible up to ~15 min; limitation: "does not prefer jumps at the beginnings or ends of structural units" — [Grachten et al., ISMIR 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000158.pdf)
- Online JumpDTW ("JOLTW"): at frames that start a part, the recursion additionally takes the minimum over the accumulated cost at the end of every part; for real-time cost it is limited to 8 transitions — "repetition of the current part; regular continuation; or jumps to the next 6 parts"; this alone raised part-wise accuracy from 10.4% to 28.3% — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Rough Position Estimator (Arzt 2010): low-res features (600 ms frames, 300 ms hop; a 10 min piece = 2,000 frames vs 30,000 high-res frames); for each new frame, greedy backward alignments of the last n = 9 s of live audio ending at every score position (max 3 steps in one direction), costs normalised and scaled to 0..1, positions with similarity >= 0.95 become hypotheses; "easily done in real time, even for pieces as long as 30 minutes"; top-1 hypothesis within 1 bar for 70% of note events, best-of-top-3 within 1 bar 83%, within 5 bars 91% — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf)
- Decision maker (Arzt 2010): 3-4 parallel fine matchers; a new matcher is initialised "not with the current time points ... but with points a few seconds in the past" (ends of the rough backward alignment) to give it context; a matcher is only judged after tracking >= 5 s; per iteration, the difference between its alignment cost and the trusted matcher's cost is summed over its lifetime and compared with thresholds: reject if sum < -r*c_t (r = 2), accept as new trusted if sum > s*c_t (s = 10); a structure model (self-similarity-derived repetition classes) prunes equivalent hypotheses, prefers "the linearly next segment", and doubles the acceptance threshold when a hypothesis is in the same repetition class as the trusted one — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf)
- Re-acquisition speed (Arzt 2010, piano, "starting in the middle" with 10-250 bars removed, 600 performances / 38 h): median time matching wrong positions before an active correct jump 7.2-10.5 s (quartile 3: 8-16 s; worst 56-69 s); with parts skipped mid-piece median 9.3-14.7 s; the old predefined-jump system usually never found the position; with the structure model, wrong or unnecessary jumps "roughly occur only once every 2 hours of a live performance" (0-2% of performances) — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf)
- Low-resolution tracker confidence (Brazier 2021): keep the last 30 LR frames (9 s) of distances to all reference LR frames, run a diagonal-matching DP that may start anywhere and may jump at part boundaries, take the minimum as the hypothesis; reliability factor rf = 1 iff the last 30 position increments lie in [15, 45] frames, i.e. the positions fit a line with slope in [0.5, 1.5]; the LR tracker alone was 85.7% part-wise accurate, but 98.9% part-wise (50.9% bar-wise, 81.7% within 5 bars) during the 63.5% of time when rf = 1; the HR tracker is reset to the LR position only when rf = 1 and the two disagree; combined JOLTW+LR = 84.0% part-wise, 51.2% bar-wise, 66.0% within 5 bars on the mutilated operas; recitatives (low harmonic similarity between recordings) remained unreliable — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Reset mechanism: "reset the HR cumulative score matrix values to the value of the LR cumulative score matrix at the LR score position. The other values are set to +inf" — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Earlier opera work used three acoustic classifiers (applause, music, speech) to "directly control the tracker in cases of spontaneous applause, stage noises, interludes, or breaks" — [Brazier & Widmer 2021, citing SMC 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Particle-filter acquisition from an unknown start: uniform prior over the whole reference converges "after a few iterations" (illustrated at k = 50 frames); an alignment is accepted only once a region of >= 15 s has a least-squares slope within 1 +/- dA and small residual, otherwise "the alignment is identified as incorrect"; with a high resampling threshold during the sparse phase so hypotheses are not lost; on real studio takes: Brahms all takes but one placed correctly (the failure was shorter than 15 s), Manoury 26/35 musical takes placed and all 14 noise-only takes correctly rejected with no false positives — [Montecchio & Cont, ISMIR 2011](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2011/paper/000028.pdf)
- HMM with arbitrary repeats/skips (audio-to-score, monophonic clarinet): real-time on ~10,000-note scores on a laptop; tracking recovered "within 0.7 s on average" after a repeat/skip — [Nakamura, Nakamura & Sagayama, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.07748)
- Multi-reference median: with n = 7 parallel OLTW trackers the system "would still come up with a reasonable position estimate" as long as n/2 + 1 stay close; "automatic replacement of trackers that got lost" is proposed but was not used — [Arzt & Widmer, ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)

### Inferences
- Map the song map directly onto JumpDTW blocks: the start/end of every part (intro/verse/chorus/solo) are the S/T jump sets. Brazier's 8-transition JOLTW handles exactly the three band cases: "repeat current part" = extended solo or extra chorus, "skip to next k parts" = dropped verse, "continue" = normal. This is cheap: per frame, the cost at each part-start cell takes an extra min over the (few) part-end cells.
- Keep a coarse tracker running at 300 ms hop over the whole song (a 4-min song is ~800 LR frames; a 30x800 DP per LR frame is microseconds). Its slope-based reliability factor (slope in [0.5, 1.5], or tighter [0.8, 1.25] given the band's +/-10% and the tapped-tempo pre-stretch) is the primary "are we lost?" signal, and its hypothesis is the re-seed target — with the STAIRS rule "prefer the linearly next equivalent part" when chorus 1/2/3 look identical.
- Expect 5-15 s to re-acquire after a structural surprise (Arzt medians 7-15 s; Brazier needs 30 LR frames = 9 s; Montecchio requires 15 s). This is the reason the tap/voice clock must remain the default owner of the clock: the tracker should bootstrap from it, nudge it when confident, and hand back on loss.
- A voice correction ("chorus", "two bars behind") is the same operation as Brazier's reset: set the accumulated-cost vector to +inf except at the seeded reference frame, and, per Arzt, seed a few seconds before the part start so the matcher has context before it is trusted again.

### Gaps
- No source reports structural-deviation recovery for a rhythm-section-driven pop band, where every chorus is nearly identical — the repetition-ambiguity problem Arzt saw in Schubert will be worse.
- Thresholds (r = 2, s = 10, rf slope bounds, similarity >= 0.95) were all tuned on classical data; they are starting points, not transferable constants.

## Q4. Hypothesis: use the band's own rehearsal recording (captured from the same interface) as the reference instead of the album

### Takeaway
The literature strongly supports it. Arzt & Widmer showed that replacing a synthesised reference with one real recording raised online accuracy on hard material (Mahler, 250 ms tolerance: 71% -> 80%; Beethoven 84% -> 85%), that the gain came despite the extra offline-alignment error, and that the band's own case — same players, same room, same gear — is the best case: the Chopin results where all 22 performances shared one piano and recording setup were called out as benefiting from exactly that. The caveats they found are the design constraints for g3xn: results "depend heavily on which performance was chosen", so the rehearsal must be a clean, full, same-arrangement take (or several, combined by median), and because lyrics/cues live on the album timeline you need one offline album-to-rehearsal alignment (synctoolbox MrMsDTW) to carry the timestamps across.

### Cited Findings
- Motivation: "we expect the quality of the features to be higher than if they were computed from a synthesised version of the score. Also, in a performance a lot of intricacies are encoded that are missing in the symbolic score, including (local) tempo and loudness changes"; "compared to the differences between a performance and an audio synthesised from the MIDI, these differences [between performances] are small" — [Arzt & Widmer, ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Results at 250 ms tolerance (offline / standard synthesised-score online / via 1 real performance / via 7 performances): Chopin Etude 99.06 / 95.62 / 97.92 / 98.78%; Chopin Ballade 97.13 / 92.10 / 96.00 / 97.93%; Mozart 99.35 / 96.88 / 97.46 / 99.04%; Rachmaninoff 96.62 / 90.14 / 87.47 / 92.47%; Beethoven 3 92.88 / 83.67 / 85.04 / 89.55%; Mahler 4 86.74 / 71.15 / 80.06 / 83.66% — [ISMIR 2015, Table 6](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- "the results also proved to be unstable. Some performances are more similar (or at least easier to align) to each other ... but the use of some of the 'score performances' led to results that were worse than our basic approach"; many errors "occurred only for one or two of the 'score performances'" — hence the 7-tracker median — [ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Same-setup effect: "we used 22 performances which were recorded by different performers, but still on the same piano and with the same recording setup, which will have a positive influence on the alignment results. Still ... even when completely unrelated performances of the same piece were used as references, the alignment results improved drastically" — [ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- "the error introduced by the off-line alignment had a lot less impact than the better quality of the features and the additional tempo and loudness information provided by the performances" — [ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Concertgebouw 2015 (Royal Concertgebouw Orchestra, Alpensinfonie): no symbolic score at all; one performance was manually annotated at downbeat level and used as the score, 6 more aligned to it automatically; live result: single reference 78.25% of downbeats within 250 ms, 92.20% within 500 ms; multi-agent 81.80% / 93.24% / 98.01% within 1 s; "there were no glitches, only some minor inaccuracies" — [ISMIR 2015, Table 7](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Structure caveat: "we chose the additional 'score performances' manually, such that they have the same structure as the piece we want to track" — [ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Opera: a commercial CD by different artists served as the reference/proxy score for live Vienna State Opera performances, annotated at bar level (5,320 bars) — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Envelope features (RMS/loudness) only work "especially when the signals were recorded in the same room and with the same musicians" — [Kirchhoff & Lerch 2011](https://musicinformatics.gatech.edu/wp-content_nondefault/uploads/2015/04/Kirchhoff_Lerch_2011_Evaluation%20of%20Features%20for%20Audio-to-Audio%20Alignment.pdf)
- MATCH's informal tests: two guitar pieces "were partially unsuccessful, due to many differences in the arrangements", i.e. arrangement mismatch, not instrument, broke alignment — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf)
- Offline album-to-rehearsal mapping tool: synctoolbox's audio-audio pipeline estimates tuning offset, computes chroma + DLNCO at 50 Hz, estimates the chroma shift on 1 Hz CENS, and runs MrMsDTW — [sync_audio_audio_full.ipynb](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/groupmm/synctoolbox/master/sync_audio_audio_full.ipynb); MrMsDTW was built for "efficient, robust, and accurate" alignment of long recordings — [groupmm/synctoolbox](https://github.com/groupmm/synctoolbox)

### Inferences
- For g3xn the rehearsal-as-reference approach removes the two biggest documented failure modes at once: structural mismatch (the band's arrangement is the reference arrangement, so the dropped verse / extended solo of *their* version is already in it) and feature mismatch (same instruments, same mix, same room, same interface, same tuning). Expect the gain to be larger than Arzt's 71 -> 80% orchestral jump, because the album-vs-cover-band gap is bigger than the gap between two professional orchestras.
- Keep the album only as the timeline for LRCLIB lyrics and user cues: run synctoolbox MrMsDTW once (album -> rehearsal) when a rehearsal is saved, store the warping path, and convert lyric/cue timestamps to the rehearsal timeline (or back). This is the one legitimate offline-alignment use in the project, and it also yields the tuning offset and chroma shift for free.
- Because a same-band reference makes envelope/onset/timbre features usable (they are useless across album vs cover), the tracker can add a cheap loudness/onset stream to chroma — useful for drum-driven sections where chroma is flat.
- Mitigate Arzt's "depends on which performance" instability by (a) rejecting rehearsal takes that are not full-length or that align badly to the album (MrMsDTW path far from diagonal in stretches), and (b) when 2-3 good rehearsal takes exist, running one OLTW per take and taking the median position (7 trackers ran in real time on a 2015 laptop).

### Gaps
- No paper tests "same ensemble, different night" references; the same-piano Chopin remark is the closest evidence that same-setup references help.
- No quantitative evidence on how much a rehearsal captured at lower quality (phone, room mic, bleed) degrades alignment vs a direct interface feed.

## Q5. Key / tuning invariance: half-step-down tunings, transposition detection

### Takeaway
Chroma handles transposition by a cyclic shift of its 12 bins; the standard way to detect the shift is to score all 12 shifts (optimal transposition index / minimum DTW cost) and pick the best, which synctoolbox implements as `compute_optimal_chroma_shift` on heavily downsampled (1 Hz CENS) features. Detuning within a semitone (A = 432-446 Hz, slightly flat Eb tunings) is handled separately by a tuning estimate that shifts the semitone filterbank, and MFCC-style timbre features are tuning-sensitive and should be avoided when key may differ. With a rehearsal reference the shift is normally zero; with the album as reference it must be estimated once per song (offline) or over the first ~10-20 s (online, at low resolution).

### Cited Findings
- `compute_optimal_chroma_shift` tries each of `np.arange(0, 12)` shifts, builds a cosine-distance cost matrix with the shifted chroma, runs full DTW and keeps the shift with the lowest final accumulated cost; it warns at >= 9000 frames and recommends smoothing/downsampling first — [synctoolbox dtw/utils.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/groupmm/synctoolbox/master/synctoolbox/dtw/utils.py); the pipeline computes `quantized_chroma_to_CENS(f_chroma_quantized, 201, 50, feature_rate)` (1 Hz CENS), calls `compute_optimal_chroma_shift` on them, then applies `shift_chroma_vectors` to both the chroma and DLNCO streams of the second recording; tuning is estimated per recording (`tuning_offset`) before pitch features are extracted — [sync_audio_audio_full.ipynb](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/groupmm/synctoolbox/master/sync_audio_audio_full.ipynb)
- Transposition-invariant self-similarity: "transpositions can be simulated by cyclically shifting chroma features", taking the extremum over the twelve shifts (max for similarity in the notebook, min for cost in the original paper) — [FMP C4S2](https://audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/FMP/C4/C4S2_SSM-TranspositionInvariance.html), [Müller & Clausen, ISMIR 2007](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2007/paper/000047.pdf); `cyclic_shift` in libfmp (`c3s1_transposition_tuning`) rolls along the chroma axis, and the same module has a baseline tuning estimator — [FMP C3S1](https://audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/FMP/C3/C3S1_TranspositionTuning.html); the audio-matching notebook includes a "transposition-invariant matching function" — [FMP C7](https://audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/FMP/C7/C7.html)
- Cover-song practice: the Optimal Transposition Index "computes a transposition based on average energy in each semitone band"; computing the OTI along the alignment path at each iteration "gave a significant performance gain" over averaged features — [arXiv 2002.03848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.03848); OTI is a standard key-invariance control in cover identification — [Da-TACOS, ISMIR 2019](https://zenodo.org/records/3527810/files/ismir2019_paper_000038.pdf); Serrà et al. use a shift based on the global song profile — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/8da678c4-09d5-4a27-9a7e-ae0ba276545a)
- Within-semitone detuning: Kirchhoff & Lerch's "pitch chroma 1" uses trapezoid filters with a 30-cent flat zone around each semitone centre "for the case that the tuning frequency may not equal 440 Hz" — [Kirchhoff & Lerch 2011](https://musicinformatics.gatech.edu/wp-content_nondefault/uploads/2015/04/Kirchhoff_Lerch_2011_Evaluation%20of%20Features%20for%20Audio-to-Audio%20Alignment.pdf); MFCC separability dropped on the guitar set, attributed to "the sensitivity of the MFCC features to the guitar tuning" — [Grachten et al. 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000158.pdf)

### Inferences
- Eb tuning against a standard-tuned album = chroma shift of -1 (11); a capo'd or transposed-for-the-singer version = any shift. Estimate it offline when the song is prepared (album vs rehearsal) and store it with the song; online, the coarse tracker can evaluate all 12 shifts over its first 30-60 LR frames (12 x 800 x 30 cells = trivial) and lock the shift before the fine tracker is trusted.
- Always run a tuning estimate on the live stream's first seconds (libfmp/synctoolbox baseline or a 12-bin peak-offset histogram) and build the chroma filterbank at that reference frequency; a band that tunes to a slightly flat Eb is otherwise half-way between bins.
- Do not use MFCC for a band that may play in a different key; chroma + onset features are invariant after the shift.

### Gaps
- No published online (streaming) transposition detector for alignment was found; the online 12-shift LR scan above is an inference from the offline OTI/chroma-shift practice.

## Q6. Guitar-only feed: does alignment survive sections where the guitar is silent or improvising?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is absent; the closest findings say that during pauses the OLTW forward path "randomly wanders", that pauses carry no alignment information, that the particle-filter followers needed an explicit loudness-gated rest model to avoid misalignment (Mozart misalign rate 14.3% -> 1.9%), and that arrangement differences (an improvised solo is one) broke MATCH on guitar pieces. The workable pattern is: gate the tracker on input loudness and let the tapped-tempo clock coast through silence; mark solo parts in the song map as "free-running" so the tracker is not trusted there and re-acquires at the next part boundary; and prefer a reference captured from the same guitar feed (rehearsal) so the features actually match.

### Cited Findings
- "our forward path tends to 'randomly' wander around during long pauses between note onsets" — [Arzt & Widmer, SMC 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_SMC_2010.pdf)
- "Pauses within the audio files pose a special problem for a content based alignment algorithm because there is no information by which the alignment could be accomplished ... the best way for the alignment path ... would be to go straight from the preceding to the subsequent non-pause content"; pause frames were defined as power below -60 dBFS (decoded) — [Kirchhoff & Lerch 2011](https://musicinformatics.gatech.edu/wp-content_nondefault/uploads/2015/04/Kirchhoff_Lerch_2011_Evaluation%20of%20Features%20for%20Audio-to-Audio%20Alignment.pdf)
- Rest handling: a loudness (SPL) feature detects rests; the score model gets sounding/resting states so the follower tolerates "sustaining notes during rests, and pauses where notes should be played"; on Mozart sonatas ("many longer rests") the mean misalign rate fell from 14.29% (no extensions) to 1.92% (rest + tempo model) and the miss rate from 35.28% to 0.12% — [Korzeniowski et al., ICMC 2013](https://phenicx.upf.edu/system/files/publications/Korzeniowski_ICMC_2013.pdf)
- MATCH's large errors "occur at the beginnings and ends of files" and "frames where no new notes appear have very little to distinguish them"; detecting silence or the first/last note "could avoid many of these errors" — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf); guitar alignments of Asturias and Spanish Dance No. 4 "were partially unsuccessful, due to many differences in the arrangements" — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf)
- Opera recitatives, where acoustic similarity between recordings is low, were the section type the LR tracker "generally does not retrieve the right position" in — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1); earlier opera work routed around non-musical passages with applause/music/speech classifiers — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- OLTW's slope limit and tempo model only coast correctly if the recent path is right; Arzt's tempo estimate deliberately ignores the last 1 s and uses 20 onsets over a 3 s window because "the correctness of the alignment of these last onsets generally is quite uncertain" — [SMC 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_SMC_2010.pdf)

### Inferences
- A guitar-only feed against the album mix is the worst case (timbre, harmony, and content all differ; the guitar is silent or doubling vocals for long stretches). Against a rehearsal reference recorded from the same feed it becomes a normal same-source audio-to-audio problem for the parts the guitar actually plays.
- Implement an explicit "no information" state: when input RMS is below a floor (e.g., -50..-60 dBFS relative to the song's level) or chroma flatness is high, freeze the tracker's position update, emit confidence = 0, and let the clock run at the last estimated rate (or the tapped rate). This is the g3xn equivalent of Korzeniowski's rest state and Brazier's classifier gating.
- Improvised solos: the song map already knows where they are; mark them as free-running (tracker advisory only, no cues fired from the tracker), and require the LR tracker's reliability before the fine tracker is trusted again at the next part.
- If the interface can carry a stereo pair, feed the tracker the room/mix channel and keep the guitar DI for the tuner/tone features; the band mix is the far better alignment signal.

### Gaps
- No paper evaluates tracking a single instrument's feed against a full-mix reference, or an improvising instrument against a fixed reference; the recommendations above are engineering inferences from rest/pause handling in classical followers.

## Q7. End-to-end design: feature rate, emission rate, smoothing, confidence thresholds, handoff to tap/voice, voice re-seeding, CPU budget

### Takeaway
A two-level tracker in pure numpy fits comfortably beside faster-whisper: a 50 fps chroma+onset fine OLTW inside a 10 s band pre-stretched by tapped_bpm/album_bpm with part-boundary jumps from the song map, plus a 3.3 fps coarse whole-song matcher that provides acquisition, lost-detection and a reliability flag. Emit (position, rate, confidence) every 100-200 ms; the clock keeps a constant-velocity Kalman state and treats tracker positions as measurements whose variance is scaled by (1 - confidence); the tracker only takes the clock when confidence has been high for >= 3-5 s and hands it back (or freezes) when it drops, with hysteresis. Measured per-frame costs from the literature put the whole thing under ~10% of one laptop core.

### Cited Findings
- Per-frame costs (matchmaker, averaged across three machines): chroma 3.05 ms, LSE 0.91 ms per frame; OLTWArzt step 0.07 ms (Cython), OLTWDixon 1.22 ms (Python); evaluation frame rate 30 fps, streaming example 50 fps (`hop_length=441` at 22050 Hz) — [arXiv 2510.10087](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10087v1), [GitHub README](https://github.com/pymatchmaker/matchmaker)
- OLTW with c = 500 frames at 20 ms hop ran "comfortably in real time" on a 2005 3 GHz PC; offline, ~10 s per minute of music — [Dixon 2005](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2005/ijcai05.pdf); 7 parallel OLTW trackers "can still be easily computed in real-time on a conventional consumer laptop" (2015) — [Arzt & Widmer, ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf); the coarse tracker is "easily done in real time, even for pieces as long as 30 minutes" — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf); a 100k-particle PF ran at ~26x real time on one 2.4 GHz i5 core — [Montecchio & Cont, ISMIR 2011](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2011/paper/000028.pdf)
- Tempo pre-stretch is exactly what Arzt's tempo model does continuously: it alters "the sequence of feature vectors representing the score audio" by inserting or removing vectors according to the relative tempo — [SMC 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_SMC_2010.pdf); MATCH suggests "computing a default slope (relative tempo) from the durations of the audio files, and biasing the forward algorithm to favour this slope" — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf)
- Smoothing: Dixon notes "the output from the DTW algorithm is not at all smooth at the local level, but we perceive most tempo changes as being smooth", and that naive smoothing worsened numerical results while interpolation removed outliers — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf); the particle-filter state (position, tempo) with Gaussian transition noise is a constant-velocity model whose decoded output is the posterior mean — [Montecchio & Cont, ISMIR 2011](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2011/paper/000028.pdf); matchmaker's `get_current_position()` extrapolates `pred_y = by + dx * tempo` from a short backward path when a tempo model is active — [oltw_arzt.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pymatchmaker/matchmaker/develop/matchmaker/dp/oltw_arzt.py)
- Trust gating in practice: decide on a matcher only after it has tracked >= 5 s; accept/reject on summed cost differences with r = 2, s = 10 ("a good trade-off between fast response times and overall robustness") — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf); trust the coarse tracker only when 30 consecutive LR positions (9 s) have slope in [0.5, 1.5] — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1); accept a PF alignment only after a >= 15 s straight-line region — [Montecchio & Cont, ISMIR 2011](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2011/paper/000028.pdf)
- Re-seeding: reset the fine tracker's cost vector to the coarse tracker's cost at the new position and +inf elsewhere — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1); initialise a new matcher "a few seconds in the past" so it has context — [STAIRS 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_STAIRS_2010.pdf)
- Median-of-trackers combination: trackers err in both directions ("running ahead" and "lagging behind") with similar frequency, so the median of 7 is robust — [Arzt & Widmer, ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Accuracy targets achieved by production OLTW on orchestral music: 81.8% of downbeats within 250 ms, 93.2% within 500 ms, 98.0% within 1 s (live concert) — [ISMIR 2015, Table 7](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)

### Inferences
Recommended design (all numbers are starting points):
1. Capture: reuse the daemon's sounddevice InputStream; add a second consumer that pushes 1024-sample blocks into a queue for a tracker thread (do not run the tracker in the audio callback). Mono mix if available, else guitar.
2. Reference prep (offline, per song, when a rehearsal is saved or the album is chosen): resample to 22.05 kHz; estimate tuning; compute 50 fps L1-normalised chroma (2048-pt STFT, log compression) and a 12-bin half-wave-rectified spectral-difference (DLNCO-like) stream; if the reference is the rehearsal, run synctoolbox MrMsDTW album -> rehearsal (feature_rate 50, step_weights [1.5, 1.5, 2.0]) to map lyric/cue times, and store the chroma shift. Store the reference feature matrix (a 4-min song = 12,000 x 24 float32 = 1.2 MB) and the song map part boundaries as frame indices.
3. Fine tracker (50 fps): OLTW on the concatenated features (Manhattan or cosine distance), band c = 500 frames (10 s), MaxRunCount 3, step weights (1, 1, 2) or synctoolbox's (1.5, 1.5, 2.0); before the song starts, pre-stretch the reference time axis by album_bpm / tapped_bpm (resample the feature matrix) so the expected path is diagonal; part-boundary jump transitions (repeat-current, continue, skip 1-6) per JOLTW; backward-path slope over the last 3-5 s gives the rate; apply the chroma shift if non-zero.
4. Coarse tracker (every 300 ms): 600 ms-smoothed, 300 ms-hop versions of the same features; distance from the last 30 coarse frames to all reference coarse frames; diagonal-matching DP with free start and part-boundary jumps; hypothesis = argmin; reliability rf = slope of the last 30 hypotheses in [0.8, 1.25] (band tempo tolerance) and low residual. Prefer the linearly-next equivalent part when choruses tie.
5. Confidence (0-1) per emission, combining: (a) normalised-cost margin in the fine window (best cost vs the median cost of the band, or vs the second-best local minimum >= 2 s away); (b) fine-path slope within [0.8, 1.25]; (c) fine/coarse agreement within +/-1 bar; (d) input loudness above the silence floor; (e) STAIRS-style summed cost differences if several fine trackers (multiple rehearsal takes) run. Two thresholds with hysteresis: take control at >= 0.7 sustained for 3-5 s, release at < 0.4 for 2 s.
6. Emission every 100-200 ms: `(position_s, rate, confidence)` -> clock. The clock keeps a constant-velocity Kalman state (position, rate) driven by the tapped/album rate as the process model; tracker measurements update it with variance proportional to (1 - confidence)^2; when confidence is 0 the clock coasts exactly as today. Interpolate rather than smooth the raw path (Dixon). Fire cues from the clock, not from raw tracker jumps.
7. Handoff: the tap/voice clock remains the owner until the tracker's confidence has been sustained; small disagreements (< 1 bar) are nudges, large ones require the coarse tracker's rf = 1 or a voice command. On loss, keep the last good rate for up to ~2 bars, then fall back to the tapped rate; show "listening..." in the stage view.
8. Voice re-seed ("chorus", "two bars behind", "we're at '<lyric>'"): compute the target reference frame from the song map / lyric line, reset the fine tracker's accumulated-cost vector to +inf except a small window around (target - 3 s), reset the coarse tracker's history, and lower the trust timer so the tracker can re-take control within ~2-3 s once its slope and cost margin look right.
9. CPU: per 20 ms frame, one 2048-pt FFT (~50 us), chroma fold (~20 us), a 500-cell band update (~50-100 us vectorised numpy, 0.07 ms in Cython); per 300 ms, a 30 x 800 DP (~1 ms in numpy). Total well under 10% of one core; faster-whisper's transcription bursts will not starve it if the tracker thread has its own queue. Avoid numba/JIT in the live path (startup latency, Windows wheel risk); keep it numpy-only.

### Gaps
- No published Kalman/particle smoothing numbers for OLTW output in a lyric-scrolling context; the Kalman/hysteresis parameters above are untested design choices.
- Windows audio-input latency of the sounddevice/PortAudio stack (WASAPI shared vs exclusive) was not researched; it adds a fixed offset to every position estimate and should be measured once and subtracted.

## Q8. Evidence of accuracy (ms) for audio-to-audio alignment, especially online, especially pop/rock

### Takeaway
Online audio-to-audio alignment on classical piano achieves median errors of 40-100 ms, average errors of 80-190 ms, and 85-99% of events within 250 ms; orchestral/live-concert tracking gets ~80-90% within 250 ms and ~98% within 1 s; opera with heavy structural mismatch drops to ~50% bar-level. There is no quantitative benchmark at all for live pop/rock vs a studio or rehearsal reference; the only pop data point is MATCH's informal 2005 note that two Beatles songs in English and German versions aligned successfully (offline).

### Cited Findings
- Online OLTW, classical piano (22 pianists, Chopin): average 84 ms, worst 2.62 s, 92.7-98.7% of notes within 200-500 ms; offline 30 ms average — [Dixon, IJCAI 2005](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2005/ijcai05.pdf); median 40 ms online vs 20 ms offline — [DAFx 2005](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/details/o-kAKlRZ1QVrNYxd28nF0g)
- Offline MATCH on commercial piano CDs: mean errors 25-102 ms, median 20 ms (40 ms for one piece), maxima 0.7-8.9 s, 2 of 221 pairs failed; overall average 64 ms on CDs vs 41 ms (median 20 ms) on the controlled Bösendorfer set — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf)
- Online OLTW with tempo model (piano/orchestra/oboe quartet): overall 89.7-91.1% of notes within 250 ms; average error on matched notes 40-79 ms; Beethoven 5 orchestral 13.9-15.9% missed — [SMC 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_SMC_2010.pdf)
- Online, real-performance references: 92-99% within 250 ms on piano, 83.7-89.6% on symphonies; live Concertgebouw 81.8% / 93.2% / 98.0% within 250 / 500 / 1000 ms — [ISMIR 2015](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_ISMIR_2015.pdf)
- Online, matchmaker benchmark (classical piano, errors > 2 s excluded): OLTWArzt MAE 91-153 ms, AAE 184-300 ms, 73-85% within 500 ms; OLTWDixon similar; HMM 319-642 ms MAE — [arXiv 2510.10087](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10087v1)
- Online particle filter (piano): misalign rates (>250 ms) 1.9-15% depending on piece — [Korzeniowski 2013](https://phenicx.upf.edu/system/files/publications/Korzeniowski_ICMC_2013.pdf)
- Online opera with 33-66% of parts cut: 84.0% part-wise, 51.2% bar-wise, 66.0% within 5 bars — [Brazier & Widmer 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531v1)
- Offline, structural differences (Mozart piano): 96.4% of beats within 500 ms (NWTW) vs 91.5% (DTW) — [Grachten 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000158.pdf); JumpDTW 98.5-99.1% bar-level on Beethoven — [Fremerey 2010](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2010_FremereyMuellerClausen_PartialSync_ISMIR.pdf)
- Offline features, time-stretched mixed-genre clips (singers, orchestra+choir, chamber groups, speech): best single feature 16.9 ms mean absolute deviation (decoded), best combination median 8.2 ms with worst-pair maximum 0.88 s — [Kirchhoff & Lerch 2011](https://musicinformatics.gatech.edu/wp-content_nondefault/uploads/2015/04/Kirchhoff_Lerch_2011_Evaluation%20of%20Features%20for%20Audio-to-Audio%20Alignment.pdf)
- Pop: "Two popular Beatles songs (I Wanna Hold Your Hand and She Loves You) in English and German versions were also aligned successfully" (informal listening test, offline) — [MATCH 2005](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2005/paper/000002.pdf)
- A search for live-concert vs studio alignment benchmarks found only Concert Stitch (ISMIR 2018), which aligns multiple audience recordings of the same concert with fingerprints + DTW — a different problem — [Subramanian & Lerch, ISMIR 2018](https://ismir2018.ismir.net/doc/pdfs/182_Paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- Planning numbers for g3xn: with a rehearsal reference and the band playing its usual arrangement, expect orchestral-class performance (80-90% of beats within 250 ms, ~98% within 1 s) in steady sections, i.e. fine for lyric scrolling and acceptable for effect cues if cues are fired by the smoothed clock rather than raw tracker ticks. With the album as reference and a cover band, expect something between the orchestral and the mutilated-opera numbers, dominated by structural and feature mismatch rather than by alignment precision.
- The absence of any pop/rock benchmark means the first deliverable should be a measurement harness: record a rehearsal and a gig from the same interface, run the tracker offline in simulation mode (matchmaker's approach), and score it against tapped bar markers before trusting it live.

### Gaps
- No quantitative online (or offline) alignment accuracy for live pop/rock vs studio recordings was found in any source; every number above is classical/opera.
- No source reports accuracy as a function of audio-input latency on Windows or of search-band width for band music.
